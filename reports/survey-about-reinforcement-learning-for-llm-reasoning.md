# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: A Research Survey

## TL;DR

Reinforcement learning (RL) for LLM reasoning trains a language model to produce useful solution trajectories by scoring sampled answers or their intermediate steps, then updating the policy toward better-scoring behavior. A major practical shift is from learned value critics and preference-only feedback toward group-relative policy optimization and outcome rewards that can be checked mechanically for mathematics or code. DeepSeekMath introduced GRPO as a lower-memory PPO variant and reported math gains after RL; DeepSeek-R1-Zero illustrated a large-scale outcome-reward recipe without preceding supervised fine-tuning (SFT), while later systems such as DAPO addressed sampling diversity, zero-advantage groups, and long-response artifacts. [1][2][3][4]

The strongest and most directly verifiable evidence in these sources concerns mathematical problem solving, with emerging task-specific results in executable code and CUDA kernel optimization. Process reward models can provide step-level feedback, but they incur annotation and modeling costs; outcome verifiers are cheaper to scale but can be wrong or exploitable. [5][6][7][8] Important open questions remain: whether RL expands a model’s reasoning repertoire or mainly makes existing successful trajectories more likely, how to sustain exploration without excessive rollout cost, and how to evaluate generalization and safety beyond familiar benchmarks. [9][10][11][12]

## Background

Here, “RL for reasoning” means post-training or training in which a model samples solution trajectories, receives rewards from a checker, reward model, environment, or human-derived signal, and updates its generation policy. It includes single-turn tasks such as math and code, as well as multi-turn interaction where execution or tool feedback informs subsequent actions. The survey focuses on the reward, optimization, data, evaluation, and deployment choices that shape such systems; it is not a claim that every long chain of thought is generated or improved by RL.

PPO provides a widely used policy-gradient baseline: it alternates sampling from the environment with optimization of a clipped surrogate objective and supports multiple minibatch epochs over sampled data. [1] Reasoning tasks present a distinctive feedback opportunity: final answers in some math problems can be checked against expected results, and code can be compiled or run against tests. But final-answer feedback is sparse: it says whether a sampled solution succeeded, not where an otherwise plausible derivation failed. Step-level process supervision addresses that granularity problem, while requiring reliable judgments about intermediate steps. [5][3]

The field’s empirical claims need careful interpretation. Reported benchmark scores vary with the base model, data, sampling protocol, training recipe, and evaluator; comparisons across papers are not necessarily controlled head-to-head experiments. A review of replication work specifically flags contamination and incomplete comparability as issues in reasoning-model evaluation. [13] Hugging Face Papers listings are useful discovery and dissemination sources for relevant papers, but their summaries are not independent replications of the underlying teams’ reported results. [14][15][9][6][7][16]

## Theme 1: From PPO to group-relative and system-level optimization

PPO’s clipped policy objective is a general foundation for learning from sampled behavior, but conventional actor-critic use requires a learned value model to estimate advantages. [1] DeepSeekMath’s GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) instead samples multiple responses to one prompt and uses their relative rewards to form advantages, removing the separate learned critic. The paper reports lower memory/computation needs associated with that critic and describes a clipped objective with KL regularization against a reference policy. [2]

This design makes intuitive sense when a prompt can generate a batch of candidate solutions and reward differences within that batch are informative. It does not eliminate the need to generate and score those candidates; nor does it guarantee useful gradients when every response in a group receives the same reward. DAPO explicitly targets these limitations with Dynamic Sampling, which discards fully correct or fully incorrect groups, and adds Clip-Higher to encourage diversity, token-level policy-gradient loss, and overlong-reward shaping. The authors report 50 points on AIME 2024 for their Qwen2.5-32B system, against 30 for their naïve GRPO baseline and a cited 47 for DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B. These are paper-reported results under that setup, not a universal ranking of algorithms. [4]

DeepSeek-R1-Zero is a prominent outcome-reward recipe: it applies GRPO to a base model without prior SFT, using final-answer correctness and format reward rather than prescribing the reasoning trace. The authors describe emergent reasoning behaviors, but also identify poor readability and language mixing as shortcomings. The subsequent DeepSeek-R1 process adds cold-start reasoning data, reasoning-focused RL, rejection-sampled reasoning data and general-domain SFT, followed by further RL for helpfulness and harmlessness. [3][14] The contrast suggests that RL and supervised data are complementary design choices: a minimal-SFT experiment probes what RL can elicit from a base policy, while a staged recipe can trade some experimental purity for usability and broader behavior.

Optimization advances therefore address both the policy update and the quality of the sampled training batch. DeepSeekMath reports GRPO improvements on GSM8K and MATH for its instruction model, while DAPO’s ablations motivate adjustments to sampling, clipping, and response-length handling. [2][4] The objective-level method alone does not determine outcomes: prompt composition, reward reliability, rollout count, and implementation are part of the effective algorithm.

## Theme 2: What is rewarded—outcomes, steps, preferences, and interaction

Outcome supervision assigns credit to a completed answer; process supervision scores intermediate reasoning steps. In mathematical reasoning, “Let’s Verify Step by Step” reports that a process-supervised reward model solved 78.2% of a representative MATH subset and outperformed outcome supervision in its reported best-of-500 selection experiments. Its PRM800K dataset contains 800,000 step-level human feedback labels covering 75,000 solutions to 12,000 problems. [5] Such dense feedback can localize where a derivation goes wrong and support ranking partial solutions, but collecting and calibrating step labels is an expensive part of the approach. The large-reasoning-model survey likewise identifies the scarcity and cost of step-by-step annotations as a data bottleneck, and reviews automatically generated trajectories and inferred process scores as possible alternatives. [17]

Outcome rewards are attractive when a task admits a robust checker. DeepSeek-R1 reports deterministic answer checking for math and compilation/testing for code, and says it avoided neural outcome or process reward models on its reasoning tasks because of reward-hacking risk, retraining cost, and pipeline complexity. In its broader pipeline it describes model-based preference rewards for general data alongside format rewards, while warning that extended optimization against learned preference rewards can itself lead to reward hacking. [3] QwQ-32B’s developer account similarly describes a first RL stage for math and coding with accuracy and execution-based checks, followed by broader reward models and verifiers. Because this is a developer announcement, its reported comparisons should not be treated as independent evaluation. [8]

“Verifiable” does not mean “infallible.” A Hugging Face Papers summary of a verifier study reports that rule-based math checkers can falsely reject equivalent answers in different formats, with errors that can worsen for stronger policies; model-based verifiers in the study had higher static accuracy but were vulnerable to false positives and reward hacking during policy optimization. This is a case study in mathematical reasoning, not a universal rate for all verifiers or domains. [6] The practical choice is thus not simply sparse versus dense reward: researchers must also ask whether the reward reflects the intended behavior, whether it remains reliable on policy-generated outputs, and how it behaves under optimization pressure.

A third feedback regime arises in interactive tasks. In multi-turn coding, execution can provide correctness and performance signals that affect the next action. The Kevin paper listing reports gains in CUDA-kernel correctness and speedup over its QwQ-32B base, and says serial refinement was more beneficial than parallel sampling in its evaluation. These are specific results for a kernel-generation setup, not evidence of broad tool-use competence. [7] This illustrates the potential of environment-grounded feedback and also its dependence on the fidelity, coverage, and cost of the test environment.

## Theme 3: Data, exploration, and what RL may add

Reasoning RL depends on the examples and trajectories it can explore. DeepSeekMath pairs continued pretraining on a large math-focused corpus with RL, reporting improvements from its instruction model’s RL phase on GSM8K and MATH. [2] DeepSeek-R1’s staged pipeline combines cold-start reasoning examples and rejection-sampled outputs with later RL, in contrast to R1-Zero’s no-preceding-SFT experiment. [3] These recipes complicate simple causal claims about “RL alone”: pretrained knowledge, supervised examples, candidate generation, filtering, and RL all contribute to the final model.

The exploration question is unresolved. One study comparing base and RLVR-trained models across model families and math, coding, and visual-reasoning tasks reports higher pass@1 after RLVR, while base models can achieve higher pass@k at large k. The authors interpret this as evidence that current RLVR may improve sampling efficiency for trajectories already present in the base distribution while narrowing reasoning coverage. This is an important challenge to the claim that RL necessarily creates new reasoning strategies, but it is one study’s analysis rather than a settled consensus. [10]

Conversely, reports of improved performance with very little RL training data suggest that some capability can be elicited through reward-driven updates even from limited demonstrations. The 1-shot RLVR paper reports Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B MATH-500 accuracy rising from 36.0% to 73.6%, with its average across six math benchmarks rising from 17.6% to 35.7%; it also notes that the chosen example matters. Its evidence concerns mathematical benchmarks and does not establish comparable generalization to code or interactive tasks. [9] These results and the pass@k analysis are not necessarily contradictory: a policy may improve its probability of producing a correct answer on sampled tests while still covering fewer distinct solution paths under extensive sampling. Resolving the issue requires evaluating both success rates and diversity/coverage under controlled compute and contamination-aware test sets. [9][10][13]

Hugging Face’s Kimi k1.5 paper listing frames its work around scaling RL and exploration, but the retrieved summary does not provide technical details or quantitative results sufficient to compare its recipe here. [16] The large-reasoning-model survey discusses search and train-time compute as ways to generate trajectories, while highlighting the computational demands and need for high-quality signals. [17] Together these sources make exploration a central research axis rather than a solved side effect of policy optimization.

## Theme 4: Scaling, rollout economics, and practical training systems

RL training cost includes repeated autoregressive sampling, reward evaluation, and policy updates. GRPO removes a separately trained critic in its described formulation, but requires multiple responses per prompt to estimate relative reward. [2] DAPO reports a recipe using 512 prompts per rollout batch and 16 responses per prompt; it also reports that truncation can add reward noise and uses loss masking and a soft length penalty to stabilize handling of long outputs. These recipe details underscore that high-performance reasoning RL depends on response-level and token-level systems engineering, not only an abstract loss function. [4]

Infrastructure measurements from OpenRLHF identify rollout generation as a practical bottleneck: in the paper’s LLaMA2-7B/A100 profile, sample generation accounts for 80% of total training time. The framework separates rollout generation from model training and uses tools such as vLLM, Ray, and DeepSpeed for throughput and distributed execution. That profiling result is specific to the stated setup, but it illustrates why faster inference, continuous batching, and overlapping generation with updates can materially affect feasible experimentation. [18]

Long responses have two opposing roles: they can contain useful deliberation and allow multiple reasoning steps, but they consume sampling budget and can introduce truncation artifacts or unproductive repetition. DAPO’s overlong-reward shaping and truncation handling are explicit algorithmic responses to this issue. [4] METR’s preliminary DeepSeek-R1 evaluation also describes long, rambling reasoning that can repeat work and waste tokens under its task setup; it emphasizes that its elicitation was moderate and its 69-task evaluation does not establish a capability ceiling. [12] Thus, “more reasoning tokens” is not automatically equivalent to better reasoning; efficiency and task-appropriate stopping belong in the evaluation and reward design.

## Theme 5: Evaluation, alignment, and limits of current evidence

Math and code are especially amenable to automatic checks, so they dominate many RLVR demonstrations. But benchmark improvement alone is insufficient evidence for broad reasoning progress. The replication survey notes risks from benchmark contamination and dependence between training and evaluation data, as well as confounding from different base checkpoints, training data, and protocols. [13] A careful comparison should disclose the base model and data, reward implementation, sampling budget, aggregation method, and evaluation contamination controls. It should also distinguish single-sample accuracy from best-of-N/pass@k, since the latter changes the test-time compute budget and can reveal changes in coverage as well as probability of success. [10][13]

Evidence in coding and agent tasks is promising but bounded. DeepSeek-R1 describes compiler and test-based code rewards, while QwQ-32B’s announcement discusses tool-related abilities; Kevin reports environment-feedback gains on CUDA kernels. [3][7][8] These sources illustrate distinct setups and evidence types, rather than demonstrating a single, standardized measure of general-purpose reasoning or tool use. METR’s task-specific evaluation further cautions that elicitation and scaffolding influence observed performance and that its preliminary task suite is limited. [12]

Reasoning capability and safety are coupled. The o1 system card describes large-scale RL and deliberative alignment, and emphasizes both possible safety benefits and risks associated with increased capabilities; it treats chain-of-thought monitoring as conditional on the faithfulness and legibility of reasoning traces, not as an established guarantee. [11] DeepSeek-R1 also reports separate helpfulness and harmlessness reward targets in its later pipeline. [3] These reports support a research agenda in which safety evaluations and reward robustness are part of reasoning-RL design, rather than downstream checks applied only after capability training.

## Trends and open problems

1. **Better feedback, not just bigger rewards.** Outcome verifiers scale when answers are objectively checkable, but false negatives, false positives, formatting sensitivity, and reward hacking can distort optimization. Process rewards offer finer credit assignment at the cost of annotation or learned-verifier reliability. Combining outcome checks with calibrated step-level or uncertainty-aware feedback is a natural direction, but the notes do not establish one universally superior scheme. [5][3][6][17]
2. **Exploration versus concentration.** Group-relative updates and dynamic sampling make useful comparisons possible, yet identical-reward groups have little relative signal, and evidence suggests RLVR may concentrate probability on existing successful paths rather than increase coverage. Future comparisons should measure pass@1 and broad pass@k/trajectory diversity, control compute, and track behavior across training rather than rely on a single score. [4][10]
3. **Economical rollout and interaction.** Sampling is a major training cost, and candidate multiplicity, long outputs, and tool interaction compound it. Better generation throughput, adaptive sampling, useful stopping, and efficient multi-turn feedback are system-level research questions as much as algorithmic ones. [4][7][18][12]
4. **Generalization beyond narrow checkers.** Math answers and tested code provide stronger automated signals than open-ended reasoning, but the available reports do not establish that benchmark gains transfer uniformly across domains. One-example RLVR and CUDA-kernel results should be read as specific demonstrations, while independent, contamination-resistant, cross-domain evaluation remains essential. [9][7][12][13]
5. **Safety under optimization pressure.** Reward hacking can appear in both learned rewards and verifiers; stronger reasoning may improve some safety behaviors while raising capability-linked risks. Reward robustness, refusal and harmlessness under multi-step reasoning, monitoring limits, and external evaluation should be evaluated alongside task performance. [3][6][11]

Overall, RL is a practical family of methods for shifting the distribution of LLM-generated reasoning toward trajectories that receive favorable feedback. The most credible near-term progress lies in matching optimization to the task’s feedback structure, validating the reward against adversarial model outputs, and reporting compute- and coverage-aware evaluations. Whether these methods reliably broaden reasoning capabilities beyond what pretraining already makes sampleable remains a central empirical question. [9][10][13]
