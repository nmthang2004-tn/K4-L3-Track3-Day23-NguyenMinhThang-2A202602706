# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Research Survey

## TL;DR

Small language models (SLMs) are not simply large models with fewer parameters: strong compact systems arise from co-design across data, architecture, training, runtime, and target hardware. Recent surveys commonly scope SLMs to decoder-only Transformers of roughly 100M–5B parameters and show that “efficient” must be measured across capability, prefill/first-token latency, decode rate, memory, and energy—not parameter count alone.[1][2] At training time, curated and staged data can make small models unusually capable in targeted domains, while compact architectures such as grouped-query attention, embedding sharing, and layer sharing trade parameter or cache cost against quality.[3][4][5][6]

At inference time, the most complementary techniques address distinct bottlenecks: low-bit weight quantization reduces model storage and memory traffic; optimized attention kernels reduce data movement; speculative decoding reduces serial generation steps; and paged KV caches plus continuous batching improve serving utilization.[7][8][9][10][11] These techniques are not interchangeable, and their gains depend on workload, hardware, kernels, and quality constraints. Recent Hugging Face-indexed work extends the design space with sparse attention and hardware co-design, including end-device systems combining several methods.[12][13][14] The central research challenge is therefore joint optimization and honest end-to-end evaluation, with safety and reliability treated as deployment requirements rather than assumed benefits of small size.[2][15]

## Background

Autoregressive language models incur two different inference workloads. **Prefill** processes the input prompt and builds attention state; **decode** generates output tokens sequentially while reusing a key/value (KV) cache. Long prompts can make prefill a dominant cost, while decode is often constrained by memory bandwidth and repeated cache access. SLM evaluations that separate time to first token/prefill from per-token decode latency consequently provide more useful deployment information than a single latency figure.[1][2]

The small-model label is relative and operational. One influential survey covers 70 open decoder-only models from 100M to 5B parameters; an edge-deployment study evaluates 68 models on task quality and runtime across two development boards.[1][2] Their scope highlights that a model’s vocabulary, layer layout, attention design, cache size, and runtime kernels all influence whether it fits a device. On-device deployment can offer low-latency local execution and data localization, but is constrained by compute, memory, energy, and heterogeneous hardware; edge–cloud collaboration offers an alternative when local execution alone is inadequate.[2][16]

The evidence base is heterogeneous. Many efficiency papers report improvements on specific model families, hardware, or benchmark setups. Their relative gains should be read as conditional results, not universal speedups. In this survey, “small” describes model scale; “efficient” describes measured task performance per resource under a specified workload.

## Themes

### 1. Data and training strategy can substitute for some model scale

A consistent lesson from compact-model research is that data quality and training schedule matter as much as parameter count. Phi-1, a 1.3B code model, combines filtered web material with generated textbook-style material and synthetic code exercises; its paper reports 50.6% pass@1 on HumanEval and 55.5% on MBPP, while the 350M phi-1-small model reports 45% on HumanEval. The result demonstrates domain-focused capability from a carefully constructed pipeline, not general parity with larger general-purpose systems.[3]

Other approaches scale data and training in different ways. TinyLlama trains a 1.1B model on about 3T tokens, using SlimPajama and StarCoder data, while MobileLLM focuses on architectural efficiency below one billion parameters. SmolLM2’s 1.7B model is trained on about 11T tokens with staged web, math, code, and instruction data, and MiniCPM reports a warmup-stable-decay schedule and a high data-to-model ratio for 1.2B and 2.4B models.[4][5][6][17] Together these projects illustrate that small inference footprints can require substantial upstream training compute. The deployment-time savings do not make model development intrinsically cheap.

Data-centric training and distillation should also be distinguished. Curated domain-relevant data can improve task-specific results, while distillation transfers information from a teacher to a smaller student; outcomes depend on the teacher, target tasks, data, and training protocol.[1][3][6] More tokens or synthetic examples are not a universal recipe: finite data quality, specialized capability gaps, evaluation leakage risks, and training cost all constrain scaling. Benchmark claims that a compact model is “on par” with selected larger models are tied to a paper’s chosen tasks and should not be read as broad equivalence.[6][17]

### 2. Compact architecture targets the active inference bottleneck

Parameter count is only one component of inference cost. MobileLLM combines deep-and-thin layers, grouped-query attention (GQA), embedding sharing, and block-wise layer sharing. Its reported experiments show parameter savings from sharing embeddings and fewer key/value heads, with corresponding small task-quality changes that can be recovered in some configurations by reallocating parameters to additional layers.[4] TinyLlama also uses GQA, which shares key/value representations across query heads and reduces KV-state requirements relative to a comparable multi-head arrangement.[5] This is especially relevant to autoregressive decoding, where the cache is repeatedly read and can become a substantial memory burden.

Architecture-level efficiency differs from post-training compression. Weight sharing, smaller KV representations, and narrower or deeper layouts change the model’s computational structure; quantization instead reduces numerical precision of stored weights, while pruning or sparse execution seeks to skip work. Their effects on accuracy, memory, and speed therefore differ and depend on implementation. Surveys of on-device systems describe these as complementary tool families, alongside hardware acceleration and edge–cloud collaboration.[1][16]

Recent hardware co-design work makes the systems dependency explicit. PLM, a 1.8B peripheral model, uses Multi-head Latent Attention to reduce KV-cache footprint and squared ReLU to encourage activation sparsity, and evaluates across several device classes and precisions.[14] This does not mean any one architecture runs optimally everywhere: mobile SoCs, laptop GPUs, and small edge boards have different memory hierarchies and supported kernels. The important design question is which resource is binding for a specific deployment—weights, KV cache, compute, bandwidth, or energy.[2][14]

### 3. Inference optimization is a portfolio, not one “speedup”

**Quantization** lowers weight storage and can reduce memory movement, but nominal bit-width is not itself a latency guarantee. AWQ uses activation statistics to identify salient weight channels and protect them during low-bit weight-only quantization; the paper reports that its TinyChat implementation combines quantized weights with kernel fusion and platform-aware packing, and reports over 3× speedup over an FP16 Hugging Face implementation in its evaluated desktop and mobile GPU settings.[7] The key synthesis is that quantization’s practical benefit depends on kernels and packing: dequantization overhead can erase theoretical gains if it is not integrated efficiently into computation.[7]

**Attention kernels** attack the cost of moving intermediate data. FlashAttention uses an exact attention algorithm designed around GPU memory hierarchy, avoiding the materialization of large attention matrices; FlashAttention-2 improves work partitioning, sequence parallelism, and non-matrix-multiplication overhead. Its reported speedups are kernel- and hardware-specific, but the method illustrates an important distinction: reducing memory traffic can improve speed without approximating the attention result.[10]

**Speculative decoding** attacks serial dependence rather than weight or cache size. A cheaper draft proposes multiple tokens, and the target model verifies them in parallel. The original method’s accept/reject-and-correct procedure preserves the target distribution without target-model retraining; its paper reports 2×–3× acceleration on T5-XXL relative to a specified T5X baseline.[8] Its realized value depends on useful proposals and the costs of drafting and verification, so it is most naturally evaluated by accepted tokens per verification step and end-to-end latency—not by the number of drafted tokens alone.

**Serving systems** address memory fragmentation and request scheduling. PagedAttention maps KV-cache blocks into non-contiguous storage, reducing waste and enabling flexible sharing; vLLM’s paper reports near-zero KV-cache waste and 2×–4× throughput over particular baselines at the same latency in its evaluations.[9] Continuous or in-flight batching admits new requests as others finish, avoiding the wait-for-the-longest-request behavior of static batches; however, larger batches remain constrained by cache and device memory.[11] Thus kernel optimization, quantization, speculation, and serving improvements can stack, but their gains must be reported under consistent batch size, prompt/output lengths, hardware, and quality requirements.

### 4. End-device systems combine methods and make tradeoffs visible

End-device papers increasingly integrate multiple optimizations. MiniCPM4 presents 0.5B- and 8B-parameter variants, trainable sparse attention for long contexts, and an inference system combining sparse attention, quantization, and speculative sampling. The Hugging Face paper record describes speed advantages over Qwen3-8B on long sequences, but the retrieved abstract does not provide device-level measurements; its result should therefore be interpreted as a reported comparative claim rather than a universal end-device speedup.[12] DeltaLLM proposes training-free temporal sparsity, combining full local attention with a delta approximation outside a local window; it reports up to 60% prefill attention sparsity and around 57% across prefill and decode on evaluated models, with accuracy changes dependent on model and task.[13]

These approaches differ in where approximation enters. Speculative decoding uses exact target verification to retain the target output distribution; sparse attention modifies which attention computations are performed or approximated, making quality validation central. Hardware-aware quantization changes representation and relies on efficient low-bit kernels. Combining them may deliver complementary savings, but interactions can also shift the bottleneck—for example, reducing weight bandwidth makes cache or scheduling costs more prominent. The system-level objective must therefore include quality, first-token latency, decode throughput, peak memory, and energy, not one isolated metric.[1][2][7][8][12][13]

Deployment is also not limited to fully local inference. A small model can handle privacy-sensitive or latency-sensitive tasks locally, while cloud models serve difficult requests; surveys identify collaboration and modular deployment as approaches to balancing resources and capability.[1][16] Dynamic routing is promising but requires reliable confidence or task-complexity signals, calibrated fallbacks, and careful accounting of communication latency and data exposure. These remain engineering and evaluation challenges rather than settled solutions.[2][16]

### 5. Evaluation and safety are part of efficiency

A rigorous evaluation should report (1) task quality and the precise benchmark/protocol; (2) prefill or time-to-first-token latency; (3) decode latency per token and throughput; (4) peak memory including weights and KV cache; and (5) energy where possible. It should specify hardware, software stack, precision, batch/concurrency, prompt and output lengths, and whether numbers are measured on-device. SLM surveys explicitly separate prefill from decode and report runtime/memory measurements; edge evaluations also caution that architecture and vocabulary affect runtime, while long personalized contexts can make prefill important.[1][2]

Safety cannot be inferred from size or local execution. An evaluation of 63 SLMs across eight jailbreak methods reports substantial vulnerability among tested models, including 47.6% with jailbreak attack success rates above 40%; the authors also find that tested prompt-level defenses are not perfect across models and attacks.[15] Those results are specific to the evaluated suite, but they establish that compact deployment requires red-teaming, policy evaluation, and robust update/fallback strategies. Local processing can improve data control, yet it does not by itself ensure safe outputs.[16][15]

## Trends and open problems

1. **Joint model–runtime–hardware optimization.** Architecture, quantization format, cache representation, kernels, and accelerator capabilities should be optimized together. A model with fewer parameters may still be slower if its operations map poorly to the target device; cross-device evaluations such as PLM’s point toward more explicit co-design.[2][14]
2. **Context-efficient inference.** Longer prompts increase prefill work and cache memory. Sparse attention, latent or compressed KV representations, cache management, and selective context processing are active directions, but comparisons need common long-context quality and latency tests.[2][9][12][13]
3. **Quality-aware composability.** Stacking low-bit weights, sparse attention, and speculative decoding could yield larger system benefits, but each changes a different part of the workload and their interaction is not guaranteed to be additive. Open work includes deciding which optimization to activate per request and detecting when approximation harms task quality.[7][8][12][13]
4. **Data-efficient capability and training economics.** SLMs can be trained on many tokens or high-quality specialized mixtures to reduce inference footprint, but the total lifecycle cost includes data generation/curation and full training. Better causal comparisons of data quality, token count, synthetic data, distillation, and architecture are needed beyond model-specific benchmark victories.[1][3][6][17]
5. **Representative evaluation.** Community reporting should standardize prefill/decode, memory, throughput, energy, concurrency, thermal conditions, and quality, and should publish device-level results. Benchmark coverage should include in-context learning and long-context behavior, where surveys identify capability limits and runtime bottlenecks.[1][2]
6. **Reliability and safety on-device.** Small local models need systematic robustness testing, calibrated refusal behavior, update paths, and safe escalation to stronger systems. Evidence of jailbreak weaknesses shows why privacy and availability benefits must be evaluated alongside harm reduction and reliability.[15]

The field is moving from “make the model smaller” toward **fit the complete inference workload to a resource envelope**. The most credible gains will come from matching an optimization to a measured bottleneck, disclosing its quality and hardware conditions, and evaluating the complete deployed system rather than extrapolating from parameter count or isolated kernel benchmarks.
